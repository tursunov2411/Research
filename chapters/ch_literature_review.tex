\section{Literature Review: Re-evaluating GVCs, the State, and Structural Transformation}
\label{sec:literature_review}
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The analytical framework of this dissertation draws upon three interconnected, and often competing, streams of political economy and development literature: (i) Global Value Chain (GVC) theory and the mechanics of industrial upgrading \citep{gereffi2005, milberg2013, humphrey2002, sturgeon2008}; (ii) developmental state theory, the East Asian industrialisation experience, and the rebirth of industrial policy \citep{amsden1989, wade1990, evans1995, chang2002}; and (iii) the structural constraints facing landlocked developing countries (LLDCs) within the context of Eurasian integration and the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) \citep{limao2001, arvis2016, carrere2004}.

By synthesising these theoretical domains, this chapter establishes the conceptual foundation for evaluating whether Uzbekistan's post-2017 liberalisation trajectory represents a genuine structural transformation or merely a reconfiguration of its historical commodity dependence. It critically examines the contradictions inherent in pursuing state-led development within deeply integrated, transnational production regimes, and engages directly with contemporary challenges to the feasibility of the manufacturing-led convergence path.

\subsection{Global Value Chains: Governance, Upgrading, and Contemporary Reconfigurations}

The globalisation of production networks over the past four decades has fundamentally altered the paradigm of industrial development \citep{gereffi1994, sturgeon2008}. Rather than building entire, vertically integrated domestic supply chains, developing economies now pursue industrialisation by inserting themselves into specific nodes of global and regional production networks \citep{gereffi2005, milberg2013}.

A critical contribution of GVC theory is the concept of ``governance''---the power dynamics dictating how financial, material, and human resources are allocated within a chain. \citet{gereffi2005} distinguish between five governance structures: market, modular, relational, captive, and hierarchy. The upgrading trajectory of a developing country depends critically on the governance structure it enters. \citet{humphrey2002} demonstrate that captive governance---where powerful lead firms strictly control specifications and limit technology diffusion---often facilitates rapid product and process upgrading but actively hinders functional upgrading (moving into design, marketing, or independent branding). Conversely, relational governance fosters deeper knowledge exchange and longer-term capability accumulation.

The ``smile curve'' phenomenon \citep{baldwin2016} further complicates GVC insertion. Value capture has increasingly concentrated at the pre-production (R\&D, design) and post-production (marketing, retail) ends of the curve, while the middle stages (manufacturing and assembly) are heavily commoditised. This structural shift has direct implications for the payoffs available to countries like Uzbekistan that are seeking entry at the assembly tier.

Recent scholarship has increasingly focused on the vulnerability and reconfiguration of these chains. \citet{rakhmatullayev2023} document how global shocks and the COVID-19 pandemic exposed the fragility of hyper-optimised GVCs, prompting lead firms to prioritise resilience over cost-efficiency. This has accelerated ``China+1'' strategies and regionalisation. For Uzbekistan, the theoretical imperative is to leverage this regionalisation to secure FDI that facilitates progression from captive assembly to relational upgrading, particularly by embedding itself into the shifting Northeast Asian production networks. \citet{usmanov2024} demonstrates that Central Asian economies that actively used industrial policy to attract export-platform FDI during GVC restructuring achieved significantly better upgrading outcomes than those that relied on passive openness.

\subsection{The Premature Deindustrialisation Counter-Thesis}

A fundamental challenge to the dissertation's central premise must be engaged directly: \citet{rodrik2016} argues that the manufacturing-led convergence path that underpinned the East Asian miracle is now structurally narrowing for late-developing countries. The ``premature deindustrialisation'' thesis identifies two reinforcing mechanisms. First, labour-saving automation is reducing the labour content of manufacturing tasks globally, compressing the employment payoff that made labour-abundant developing countries attractive as manufacturing platforms. Second, the shift of GVC value capture toward pre- and post-production segments means that countries entering at the assembly stage capture less domestic value added than their predecessors did at equivalent reform stages.

\citet{diao2019} and \citet{mcmillan2017} extend this critique empirically, demonstrating that structural change in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia has moved labour from agriculture into services rather than manufacturing, and that this ``wrong'' structural change has not generated the productivity convergence that manufacturing-led transitions produced in East Asia.

However, this critique does not invalidate the Uzbekistan case in the same terms. Uzbekistan is not primarily competing for the automatable, low-skill assembly tasks that the premature deindustrialisation thesis identifies as most vulnerable. Its strategic positioning targets medium-skill, relationship-intensive manufacturing (automotive components, pharmaceuticals, precision instruments) in a regional market where EAEU trade preferences and geographic proximity to Russia and Central Asian consumers provide a competitive basis that pure labour-cost competition cannot replicate. The premature deindustrialisation critique is most acute for large-population, low-income economies seeking mass employment absorption; for a middle-income, STEM-capable economy of Uzbekistan's profile, the constraint is governance and logistics quality rather than the structural foreclosure of the manufacturing path.

\subsection{The Developmental State and the Rebirth of Industrial Policy}

The integration of Uzbekistan into Northeast Asian networks must be analysed through the theoretical lens of East Asian industrialisation. The classical interpretation of the East Asian ``miracle'' rests on the concept of the developmental state \citep{amsden1989, wade1990, evans1995}, which challenges the neoclassical orthodoxy that East Asian growth resulted primarily from free markets and minimal state intervention.

\citet{amsden1989} demonstrated how South Korea's industrialisation relied on ``getting prices wrong''---using targeted subsidies, directed credit, and strategic protectionism to incubate domestic champions. \citet{wade1990} advanced the ``governed market'' theory, illustrating how Taiwan's state apparatus actively steered investment into strategic sectors. \citet{evans1995} introduced the concept of ``embedded autonomy,'' arguing that successful developmental states require bureaucracies that are sufficiently insulated from rent-seeking pressures (autonomy) yet deeply connected to the industrial capitalist class to facilitate policy implementation (embeddedness).

\citet{andreoni2023} document a ``rebirth'' of industrial policy globally, arguing that the theoretical case for market-led development has been substantially undermined by the GFC, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the reshoring pressures generated by US-China technology competition. For Uzbekistan, the challenge is constructing an institutional apparatus capable of exercising embedded autonomy within the constraints of modern trade rules, navigating the tension between required liberalisation and the necessary state intervention to foster domestic capabilities. \citet{rakhmatullayev2023} identify specific institutional bottlenecks --- judicial independence, contract enforcement, bureaucratic discretion in investment approvals --- that remain unresolved and which actively limit the transition from passive openness to developmental transformation.

\subsection{The Flying Geese Paradigm and South-South Cooperation}

The spatial dynamics of East Asian FDI are elegantly captured by the ``flying geese'' model \citep{akamatsu1962}. The model describes a tiered hierarchy in which a lead economy transfers sunset, labour-intensive industries to follower economies as its own comparative advantage shifts toward capital- and knowledge-intensive production \citep{dunning1981}.

Applying the flying geese model to contemporary Central Asia requires significant modification. While China is graduating from labour-intensive manufacturing, creating a theoretical opportunity for Uzbekistan, Chinese outward FDI often diverges from the classical model. As \citet{pomfret2019} notes regarding Central Asia, Chinese South-South cooperation often prioritises infrastructure and resource extraction over the export-platform manufacturing seen in the traditional flying geese cascade. The Belt and Road Initiative, while providing critical infrastructure, may deepen primary commodity dependence if not paired with robust domestic industrial policies.

\subsection{Comparative Development Benchmarks: Vietnam and Thailand}

Uzbekistan's aspirations can be benchmarked against two successful models of GVC insertion: Thailand and Vietnam. Thailand's Eastern Seaboard Development Programme leveraged Japanese FDI following the 1985 Plaza Accord. The state successfully utilised local content requirements to force technology transfer, establishing Thailand as a regional automotive hub.

Vietnam represents a contemporary model of rapid GVC embedding following the Doi Moi reforms and its 2007 WTO accession. Vietnam successfully positioned itself as a ``China+1'' destination, attracting massive electronics assembly FDI through a combination of institutional credibility, anchor investor targeting (Samsung at Thai Nguyen), and calibrated SEZ infrastructure. Vietnam's trajectory underscores that trade liberalisation alone is insufficient; the sequencing of anchor investor attraction, domestic supplier development, and human capital calibration is what converted policy openness into productive GVC embedding.

\subsection{Central Asian Structural Constraints and Landlocked Theory}

Uzbekistan's integration prospects are uniquely bounded by its physical geography. \citet{limao2001} demonstrate that landlocked countries face a significant structural penalty, with transport costs averaging 50\% higher than coastal economies. For a doubly landlocked nation, this geographical penalty precludes integration into time-sensitive, low-margin GVCs.

These structural constraints dictate a specific industrialisation pathway. High-volume, low-value assembly is economically unviable due to prohibitive transit costs. Industrial upgrading must target goods with high value-to-weight ratios or leverage regional markets where transit penalties are minimised. The political economy of the Middle Corridor and the China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway are therefore existential prerequisites for Uzbekistan's GVC embedding. \citet{worldbank2025} emphasises that multimodal transport efficiency and corridor governance remain the binding constraint on export competitiveness for Central Asian economies, noting that Uzbekistan's Logistics Performance Index score of 2.6 places it 14\% below the lower-middle-income country average, with transit-time unpredictability identified as the primary deterrent by surveyed export manufacturers.

\subsection{Technology Transfer and FDI Spillovers}

The final theoretical piece concerns how FDI translates into domestic capability. Theoretical models of FDI spillovers suggest that foreign presence can increase domestic productivity through demonstration effects, labour mobility, and vertical linkages \citep{dunning1981}. However, empirical evidence is mixed. Without sufficient ``absorptive capacity'' (human capital, R\&D investment) in domestic firms, foreign presence can crowd out local enterprises rather than upgrading them. Thus, SEZ policies in Uzbekistan must be explicitly tied to domestic supply-chain integration rather than operating as isolated enclaves.

The critical policy implication emerging from this literature is one that directly informs the empirical analysis: the mechanism through which external engagement produces manufacturing upgrading is not FDI volume per se, but intermediate goods trade deepening. Countries that develop dense backward linkages --- sourcing intermediate inputs domestically rather than importing them --- generate the technology transfer and productivity spillovers that aggregate FDI statistics cannot capture. This distinction between FDI quantity and FDI quality, and between investment attraction and production network embedding, frames the empirical investigation.

\subsection{Synthesis: Economic Complexity and Upgrading}

To measure whether these varied policy efforts are translating into genuine structural transformation, this study relies on the Economic Complexity Index (ECI) \citep{hidalgo2009}. The ECI posits that development is fundamentally a process of accumulating productive knowledge. \citet{andreoni2023} further elaborate on the policy implications of economic complexity, arguing that industrial policy must be directed toward building capabilities in strategically proximate, more complex industries to avoid the ``complexity trap'' of commodity dependence --- a trap that Uzbekistan's current gold-and-cotton export profile directly exemplifies. \citet{akramov2024} extend this argument to the agricultural value chain context, finding that Central Asian economies that successfully diversified into agro-processing demonstrated measurably higher ECI trajectories than those that remained commodity-dependent, a finding with direct implications for Uzbekistan's nascent horticulture processing sector.

\subsection{Research Gap}

The existing literature exhibits three critical gaps. First, while extensive scholarship analyses Southeast Asian GVC integration, the mechanisms of LLDC integration into Northeast Asian networks from a double-landlocked position remain under-theorised. Second, empirical studies on Uzbekistan overwhelmingly focus on macroeconomic liberalisation rather than disaggregated GVC governance structures. Third, comparative benchmarking between Central Asian and ASEAN industrial models that accounts for the double-landlocked geography is largely absent. This dissertation addresses these gaps by quantifying the structural asymmetries in Uzbekistan's trilateral engagement and positioning the captive/relational governance distinction as the analytical lens for policy evaluation.
